# John 8: Abraham and the I-am saying

This bounded reading for #381 records source attribution. It supplies no
historical utterance, actual divine authority, divine identity or frontier
closure. Issue #381 remains open for historical attribution and rival defense.

The chapter moves from the opening woman/accusers scene to light, testimony,
origin, sending, judgment and the Father (8.12–30), then freedom, Abrahamic
ancestry and disputed parentage (8.31–47). At 8.28 Jesus disclaims acting from
himself and attributes his speech to the Father's teaching. At 8.40 he calls
himself a man who has spoken truth heard from God; 8.42 describes divine
sending. These immediate controls prevent an interpretation that simply
erases the speaker/sender distinction. They do not themselves decide whether
such sending includes personal preexistence or a divine nature.

In 8.48–55 the interlocutors accuse Jesus of having a demon; he rejects the
charge, honors his Father and distinguishes the Father who glorifies him.
His saying about keeping his word and never seeing death raises the question
whether he is greater than Abraham and the prophets, who died. At 8.56 he
says Abraham rejoiced to see his day and saw it. The interlocutors frame an
age/seeing objection (8.57). Jesus answers with the before-Abraham/I-am saying
(8.58); they take stones to throw at him, and he hides and leaves (8.59).
The narrative does not explicitly name blasphemy as their motive here.

Chapter 9 narrates the blind man's healing, identity dispute, interrogation,
expulsion and encounter with Jesus. In 9.8–9 the neighbors dispute whether
the man is the former beggar; his I-am answer identifies himself. The control
is ordinary identity in its own setting. Later disputes about Jesus's origin
and the man's confession belong to the chapter's larger portrayal; neither
the healing's occurrence nor those characters' judgments is independently
verified by recording their place in this narrative.

## Positive readings and their obligations

A **personal-preexistence reading** takes the before-clause, first-person
subject, age objection and Abraham's seeing of Jesus's day together as a
claim that the speaker existed before Abraham came to be. It accounts for
the striking contrast between Abraham's beginning and the speaker's present
self-predication. To formalize this as temporal existence requires an account
of tense/aspect and the implicit predicate; the speech-form report does not
already prove it. Even personal existence before Abraham is weaker than
beginningless or necessary existence, numerical identity with the Father,
or the Creed's consubstantiality. A preexistent commissioned agent remains
a different possibility from God by identity.

A **divine-name or divine-self-disclosure reading** treats the unusual
before-clause/I-am contrast, the chapter's origin and recognition language
(including 8.24 and 8.28), and the climactic hostility as a portrayal of more
than ordinary chronological seniority. It can explain the saying as divine
self-presentation without erasing the Father/sender distinction. Its force
requires a defended intertextual account, not mere repetition of the words.
No Exodus 3 witness was materialized for this work. Any specific Exodus
comparison, wording agreement, identification with a divine name or claim
about the author's intended allusion remains pending a pinned primary
witness and contextual comparison. No such intertextual conclusion is
attributed as an established source claim here.

A **delegated-agent reading** positively explains the chapter's teaching,
sending, obedience and glorification language as an authorized revealer who
speaks from God rather than independently. It can coexist with personal
preexistence; it is not equivalent to an ordinary human prophet with no
exceptional status. To exclude divine nature from that agency requires a
further argument: speaker/sender distinction alone does not do it.

An **ideal-priority reading** instead understands priority to Abraham as
priority of the speaker's messianic identity or day within God's purpose,
with Abraham's rejoicing oriented toward that promised day. This accounts
for the day language and preserves divine commission without asserting a
person living before Abraham. Its main burden is explaining the first-person
before-clause and the age/seeing objection without silently replacing them
with an unspoken plan predicate. The materialized verses do not explicitly
say that the speaker existed only ideally in God's purpose. Consequently
this account is a rival research proposal here, not a sibling claim falsely
attributed to the verse's literal wording. No scholar is named as its source.

A **narrative-development account** accepts these textual attributions as
the Gospel's presentation while withholding historical attribution of the
saying and reaction. It needs a positive transmission, dating and development
account to become more than a possible explanation. The consulted witness
supplies no independent documentary sequence establishing that account.
These are local interpretive/historical accounts, not complete worldviews;
eventual comparison must apply the shared questions in catholic-case.md.

## Rejected shortcuts and remaining work

Every occurrence of I am is not a God claim: 9.8–9 supplies an ordinary
counter-use. Conversely that control does not show that the quite different
8.58 context lacks divine significance. Taking stones does not establish
the crowd's exact interpretation, the narrator's theological endorsement,
a blasphemy verdict or Jesus's actual identity. Their hostility is inside
the same narrative, not independent hostile attestation. The text's first
person is the reported speaker's, not a report-to-history rule. Commission,
personal preexistence, divine self-disclosure and actual authority need
separate judgments; none is silently identified with the case signature or
the Creed. A mad/bad/God inference would further require historical speech,
the required self-understanding and exclusion of delegated, ideal and
narrative-development accounts; this chapter does not provide that package.
